# Evaluating Text-to-Image Generative Models: An Empirical Study on Human Image Synthesis

**Authors:** Muxi Chen, Yi Liu (equal contribution), Jian Yi, Changran Xu, Qiuxia Lai, Hongliang Wang, Tsung-Yi Ho, and Qiang Xu.
**Year:** 2024.
**Source:** [[raw/papers/chen2024human.pdf]]; arXiv:2403.05125v2, 28 October 2024. The paper lists [code and data](https://github.com/cure-lab/EvaluateAIGC); the external repository was not independently inspected.
**Last updated:** 2026-09-22.

## Research question

How can text-to-image models be evaluated on human image synthesis while distinguishing aesthetic appeal, anatomical realism, adherence to requested concepts, and demographic bias?

## Motivation

Distribution-level scores such as FID and IS can miss local anatomical defects. Image–text similarity does not directly establish concept fulfillment, and a unified preference score can obscure which dimension improved. The authors propose separate image-quality and text-condition assessments rather than a single overall score (§§1–2).

## Method

- **Aesthetics:** CAN (CLIP-based Aesthetic score prediction Network) combines a CLIP style module, a ResNet-34 generic aesthetic module, and attention-based feature fusion. Training combines aesthetic-score regression with distortion classification. The main comparison uses AVA training; the described released attribute predictor additionally freezes the network and fine-tunes its fully connected layers on PARA (Appendix §2.2). See [[CAN aesthetic assessment]].
- **Realism:** annotate faces, bodies, and ten anatomical components, then train ViT classifiers to predict good, bad, or invisible components and face quality. See [[Anatomical defect evaluation]].
- **Concept coverage:** generate 500 images per concept; use BLIP-based VQA either to confirm a named concept or describe an action without naming it. Repeated open answers are semantically clustered using ChatGPT, with a dominant-answer match and entropy threshold determining acceptance. See [[VQA-based concept coverage]].
- **Fairness:** generate 500 images for each of 51 prompts, infer perceived gender, race, and age using VQA, and flag concentrated attribute distributions using entropy thresholds. See [[Demographic diversity and bias]].

## Datasets

| Dataset or generated collection | Role and scope |
| --- | --- |
| AVA | CAN aesthetic training and in-domain evaluation |
| PARA and TAD66K | Cross-dataset aesthetic evaluation without training on these datasets in Table 1; PARA also supplies attribute fine-tuning for the described released model |
| Annotated human defect dataset | 79,908 images in total: 10,000 SDXL generations plus real images from AISegment CLIP images, CelebAMask-HQ, and DeepFashion |
| Defect-classifier split | 2,000 generated images held out for testing; remaining images used for training (Appendix §3.6) |
| Aesthetic model comparison | Main text reports 50,000 images generated by four models; it does not clearly allocate this count per model |
| Concept coverage collection | 30 concepts: 10 actions and 20 interactions; 500 images per concept per evaluated SD model |
| Fairness collection | 51 prompts × 500 images per evaluated SD model; excludes three multi-person interaction concepts |

Aesthetic-comparison prompts have 18 possible parts spanning image style, people, scene, camera, lighting, and quality terms, with different inclusion probabilities (Figure 7). Fairness uses a simple template describing a realistic photo of a person with the target concept. These are different prompt protocols.

## Models

- **Generators:** Stable Diffusion 1.5, 2.1, SDXL, and Midjourney for aesthetics and facial defects; only the three SD models for concept coverage and fairness. The supplied text does not specify the Midjourney version.
- **Evaluators:** CAN; TANet aesthetic baseline; ViT defect classifiers; BLIP-based VQA; ChatGPT for semantic equivalence; CLIPScore baseline. Exact VQA and ChatGPT checkpoints, repeated-answer count, and full generation settings are not sufficiently specified in the supplied text for exact reproduction.
- **Diagnostic baselines:** RTMDet-l human detection and RTMPose-l pose estimation confidence, which do not reliably separate defects from occlusion or small body parts.

## Metrics

- Aesthetic predictor validation: Spearman rank correlation (SRCC) and pairwise rank accuracy; generator comparison: mean and standard deviation of predicted aesthetic scores.
- Realism: predicted face/component defect rates; classifier validation accuracy. Invisible components are a separate class, not automatically defect-free anatomy.
- $\mathrm{cov}_{closed}$: proportion of images receiving a yes answer to a concept-specific question.
- $\mathrm{cov}_{open}$: proportion whose dominant open-answer cluster matches the concept and whose answer entropy is at most 0.8.
- $\mathrm{cov}_{clip}$: thresholded CLIPScore, with a reported threshold of 0.2; score implementation must be checked before reuse.
- Human loose coverage: intended concept is present. Human strict coverage: concept is present **and the depicted human has no defects**. Strict coverage therefore combines alignment and realism.
- Bias: proportion of prompts below attribute-entropy thresholds, plus mean entropy among those flagged prompts. Thresholds are 0.8 for gender and 1.0 each for race and age.

## Main findings

**Aesthetic prediction (Table 1).** CAN versus TANet SRCC is 0.754 versus 0.755 on AVA, 0.751 versus 0.721 on PARA, and 0.643 versus 0.640 on TAD66K. Pairwise accuracy is respectively 0.780 versus 0.780, 0.681 versus 0.614, and 0.431 versus 0.420. Cross-dataset gains are largest on PARA; in-domain performance is essentially matched.

**Generator quality (Tables 3–4).** These are evaluator predictions under this study’s prompts, not universal model rankings or manually measured population defect rates.

| Generator | Mean aesthetic score | Score SD | Predicted face defect rate |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Midjourney | 6.35 | 0.45 | 29% |
| SDXL | 6.23 | 0.55 | 61% |
| SD2.1 | 5.62 | 0.57 | 79% |
| SD1.5 | 5.54 | 0.55 | 86% |

Component classifiers average 88% accuracy; the face classifier is reported at 86%. Separate face generalization tests yield 85.0%, 84.3%, 87.2%, and 86.3% for SD1.5, SD2.1, SDXL, and Midjourney. Predicted hand defects remain very frequent (SDXL 99%, SD2.1 98%; Table 9), so improvements are not uniform across components.

**Concept coverage (Tables 5 and 10).** Table 5 reports closed/open averages of 94.27%/86.50% for SDXL, 92.93%/76.27% for SD2.1, and 92.82%/77.19% for SD1.5. However, arithmetic means recomputed from the 30 printed rows in Tables 12–14 give open coverage of **81.83%, 74.49%, and 75.85%**, respectively. Closed averages reproduce Table 5. Preserve this unresolved reporting discrepancy; the ordering remains the same.

Across concepts, closed coverage correlates with loose human coverage at 0.61/0.71/0.48 for SD1.5/SD2.1/SDXL, versus 0.12/0.19/0.17 for thresholded CLIP. Open coverage correlates with strict human coverage at 0.51/0.58/0.69, versus 0.32/0.24/0.27 for CLIP. These are aggregate concept-level correlations, not per-image agreement. Reported VQA action accuracy is 95.9% for closed and 96.0% for open questions.

**Demographic concentration (Table 6).** Percentages of prompts flagged for gender/race/age bias are 51%/27%/35% for SDXL, 41%/35%/31% for SD2.1, and 51%/47%/24% for SD1.5. Across SD1.5 to SDXL, race concentration flags decrease, gender flags are unchanged, and age flags increase. Better image quality does not imply improvement across all demographic measures.

## Limitations

- The authors acknowledge incomplete evaluation coverage, imperfect defect classifiers, and restriction to single target concepts; the framework does not establish compositional alignment across multiple related concepts.
- Defect training uses SDXL generations plus real images; subjective borderline anatomy, lighting, visibility, and distribution shift affect predictions. No uncertainty intervals accompany the headline generator comparisons.
- CAN cross-dataset validation concerns aesthetic datasets; it does not by itself validate all generated-image styles or prompt-modifier conditions.
- Strict human coverage mixes fidelity with anatomical quality. Open-answer uncertainty is not a direct anatomical defect test, despite its correlation with strict judgments.
- The human validation descriptions omit enough recruitment, sample-size, and inter-rater-agreement information to limit reliability assessment. Exact evaluator configurations and repeated-answer sampling details are also incomplete.
- The main text calls evaluator group differences typically within 1% and bias negligible. Appendix Table 11 instead reports gender-classification accuracy of 82.61% for the group labeled African versus 97.18% for White and 96.04% for Asian. This substantially qualifies the claim. Its caption incorrectly describes concept-coverage correlations although the cells report attribute accuracies.
- Demographic labels are restricted model-inferred appearance categories, not verified identities. Binary gender and coarse race/age bins limit interpretation; low entropy alone does not establish unfairness, especially when the prompt explicitly specifies the attribute. Handling of those specified attributes in the aggregate bias count is insufficiently explicit.
- Table 5 open-coverage averages conflict with the appendix rows. Main-text appendix references also use section numbers that do not match the supplied appendix’s final numbering; follow named sections/tables.

## Relevance to my thesis

The strongest contribution is an evaluation design that separates [[Aesthetic quality]], [[Anatomical realism]], [[Prompt alignment]], and [[Demographic diversity and bias]]. It provides alternatives to relying on a single CLIP or preference score when evaluating [[Prompt modifiers]].

A proposed thesis adaptation is to compare modifier conditions on matched base prompts and repeated generations, reporting aesthetic scores, defect rates, concept success, and demographic distributions separately. Validate automated judges on a human-labeled subset of each relevant condition and report uncertainty. This is a proposed extension: the paper does **not** causally test individual modifiers. Its inclusion of HDR/UHD, lighting, and camera terms in prompt construction is not evidence of their isolated benefits.

Demographic entropy and aesthetic-score variability are not general image-diversity measures. Broader visual/semantic diversity requires an additional operational definition.
